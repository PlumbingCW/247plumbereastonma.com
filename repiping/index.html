Four pipe materials you may find in an older New England house

Pipe tells you the age of a house better than a deed. Open a basement ceiling or look at the water line near the meter, and you will usually see one of four materials. Each ages differently, and not all of them fail the same way.

Galvanized steel was common in older homes until copper replaced it. It rusts from the inside, so the opening narrows and pressure drops. Copper lasts for decades but can develop pinholes in some water conditions. Polybutylene, a gray plastic used roughly from the late 1970s to the mid 1990s, has a record of failures at fittings. PEX is the flexible plastic used in newer work and retrofits.

Signs your pipes are near the end

Warning signs

  • Brown or orange water when you turn on a tap after sitting idle.
  • Pressure that fades when two fixtures run at once.
  • Pinhole leaks, green stains or damp spots in walls and ceilings.
  • Visible rust or flaking on exposed galvanized pipe.
  • Repairs that keep reappearing in different places.
  • Noisy pipes or a dull taste in the water.

One sign alone is not a verdict. A cluster of them across the house is, and a plumber can confirm it with a pressure test and a look at pipe sections.

Repipe all of it or just the worst section

Not every house needs a full repipe. If one branch is corroded and the rest is sound, replacing that section may be the sensible move. A plumber should be honest about which, and show you the pipe they are judging.

Mixed systems are another reason to repipe. Many older Easton houses have copper in one wing, galvanized in another and a few plastic repairs between. Joining dissimilar metals invites corrosion at the joint, so a whole-house approach can be simpler than patching again and again.

Permits and the state code

Massachusetts requires a permit from the local plumbing inspector before plumbing is installed, altered, removed, replaced or repaired. Only licensed plumbers can pull it. Repiping is clearly beyond the minor exemptions, which cover repairing a leak in a faucet or valve and clearing a stoppage.

Ask the Easton building department which forms and inspections apply to your project. You can also verify the plumber's license on the state's online lookup. A skipped permit can cause trouble at resale and with insurers.

What a repipe means for an Easton house

Older village homes

Plaster walls and tight chases make access the main challenge. Expect careful planning and patch work.

Postwar capes and ranches

Often copper or galvanized with basement or crawlspace access, which keeps wall cutting down.

Newer subdivisions

Copper or PEX already, so repiping is usually targeted at a single failing line or manifold.

Permits and code for repiping

Repiping is permitted work, so a licensed plumber pulls the permit under 248 CMR and the local inspector checks it, according to the state plumbing code. Material choices should follow both the code and the manufacturer's installation standards.
