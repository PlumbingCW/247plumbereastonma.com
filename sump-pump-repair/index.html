Pit filling and the pump is not running: your first ten minutes

Start by looking from a dry spot. If the water is near outlets, a furnace, a water heater or the electrical panel, stay out of the basement and tell the dispatcher so the plumber arrives prepared. Standing water and power are a dangerous mix, and a pump that has been underwater can energize the water around it.

If the floor is dry and you can reach the plug safely, check that the pump is still plugged into a working outlet. A tripped GFCI outlet is a common reason a pump goes quiet, and one press of the reset button sometimes brings it back. If it restarts, you still need it looked at, because it tripped for a reason.

Move boxes, rugs and anything stored on the floor onto shelves or up the stairs. Then call. A plumber can often get a pump running or swap it the same visit, which is far cheaper than cleaning up a flooded finished basement.

What a failing sump pump sounds and looks like

Pumps rarely die without warning. These are the patterns plumbers hear about most often when they get the call.

Runs constantly

A float that sticks or a check valve that leaks lets the same water fall back into the pit, so the pump cycles all day. It wears out early and raises the electric bill.

Hums but moves no water

A seized impeller, a frozen or clogged discharge line, or a failed motor capacitor. Do not let it hum for long, because the motor will burn out.

Loud rattling or grinding

Gravel and silt chew up the impeller. Bearings wear too, and a pump with a noisy motor is usually near the end of its life.

Never turns on

The float is tangled on the pit wall or the switch has failed. Sometimes the pump is fine and the outlet is not.

Short cycling

The pit is too small, the float is set wrong or the check valve is missing. The pump starts and stops every few seconds.

Smells of sewage or rot

Stagnant water in an unused pit can smell. A covered pit lid and a working pump usually clear it up.

Why pumps quit in the storms that matter most

A pump that has sat idle through a dry stretch is not tested. Then a three-day soaking rain arrives, the pit fills quickly, and the pump has to run for hours at full load. That is when a worn float switch or tired motor gives out.

Power outages make it worse. Nor'easters and summer thunderstorms often knock out electricity at the same time the water table rises, so a pump that depends only on house power stops exactly when the pit needs it most. That is the reason plumbers talk so often about waterproofing and a battery backup as a pair.

Repair the pump or replace it?

There is no single age at which a pump should be replaced. These are the points a plumber weighs on site.

Repair makes sense when

  • The pump is fairly new and only the float switch or check valve has failed.
  • The discharge line is clogged or frozen, not the motor.
  • The pit is clean and the pump has no corrosion or cracked housing.
  • The fault is the outlet or the wiring, not the unit.

Replace makes sense when

  • The motor is seized, burned out or the housing is cracked.
  • The pump has been running nonstop for months and is noisy.
  • It is too small for how much water the pit receives.
  • You want a primary pump and a separate battery-powered backup.

A plumber will also look at the check valve, the pit liner and the discharge pipe. A new pump connected to a failing check valve will cycle just as much as the old one did.

Easton low ground and the Hockomock Swamp

The Hockomock Swamp is the largest freshwater swamp in Massachusetts, about 16,950 acres across six towns, and part of it lies in Easton. The state describes it as a natural flood control area for the region, which tells you how much water moves through the ground around it.

The practical point for a homeowner is simple. If your house sits on low ground or near wetlands and brooks, expect the groundwater under the basement to rise in spring and after long rain, and treat the sump pump as safety equipment rather than a convenience. Ask neighbors whether their pits run often, and check the pump before March, not during it.

Where the water goes matters

A working pump can still cause trouble if the discharge pipe is routed badly. Massachusetts towns commonly prohibit connecting a sump pump to the sanitary sewer, because clean groundwater overloads the system and can back up other basements.

Discharge checks for your sump line

  • The pipe empties outdoors, well away from the foundation, and drains downhill.
  • It is not tied into a sanitary sewer drain or a floor drain that feeds the sewer.
  • The outlet is not blocked by leaves, snow or ice in winter.
  • It does not send water onto a neighbor's lot or across a sidewalk where it can freeze.
  • A check valve sits above the pump so water does not fall back into the pit.

Rules for connecting to a storm drain differ between towns, so ask the Easton building department or public works before changing where a pump discharges. If your home is on septic, keep sump water away from the septic field too.
