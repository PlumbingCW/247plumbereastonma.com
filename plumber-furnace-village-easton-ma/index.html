An iron village on Foundry Street

The Furnace Village Historic District was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 6, 1983. Wikipedia describes it as centered on the junction of Foundry Street, which carries Routes 106 and 123, with South Street and Poquanticut Avenue.

The settlement dates to the early 1700s. Industry began in 1742 and an iron furnace was set up in 1751. The state historic survey adds a foundry near Old Pond, a cannon proving ground, and a fulling mill and tannery on Mulberry Brook, and notes that the village stayed relatively separate from the rest of town.

Houses that span three centuries

According to the survey, Federal to late industrial housing lines Highland Street and Foundry Street. The corner of Highland and South Streets keeps intact late colonial and Federal houses and farm buildings. The National Register listing describes colonial-era houses and 19th century worker housing.

A house of that age has usually been added to, heated, rewired and replumbed in stages. That does not mean its pipes are poor. It means the first useful step is to identify what is there, which is why a plumber should look before quoting.

What the village's age means for a repair

In many older houses the pipe you need to reach sits in a tight basement or a low crawlspace. Ask whether the plumber can get to it before you agree to a price, because access often drives the cost of a repair more than the part does.

Do not open a wall to chase a leak before the source is confirmed. For any active leak, shut off the water at the main and call. Take photos of the damage for your insurance claim.

Questions to settle before any work on an old house

Two lists help here. One is what you can find out yourself, and one is what is worth a plumber's eyes.

You can check

  • Where the main water shutoff is and whether it turns
  • Whether the house is on town sewer or a septic tank
  • Which fixtures are slow, noisy or stained
  • What the last owner or inspector reported

Worth a plumber's look

  • Repeat clogs after cleaning
  • Water stains with no clear source
  • A tank water heater that is old or leaking
  • Pipe you cannot identify in the basement

If any of the right-hand items sound familiar, leak detection and sewer inspection are the pages to read.

Septic, sewer and water questions here

We have no source that says which Furnace Village streets are on town sewer. The town's 2008 preservation plan says that, apart from Stonehill College and one condominium complex, properties relied on septic systems, but that is old and general. Ask the town or check your closing records.

If your house is on septic, a backup can mean a full tank or a failing leach field instead of a clog. The plumber should ask before running a cable. Sewer line cleaning covers what to expect.

Water comes from the Easton Water Division's wells. Its 2024 report lists treatment with lime and ultraviolet disinfection, and a plant on Red Mill Road for iron and manganese. It gives no hardness figure.

Brooks, ponds and a wet basement

Mulberry Brook powered the old mills, and Leach's Pond now lies within Borderland State Park. We do not have street-level flood or groundwater data for this village and will not guess at it.

What we can say is general. Any basement near a brook or pond is worth checking in spring, and a sump pit that has sat dry all winter should be tested. The sump pump repair page explains how to do it safely.

Roads and neighbors

Foundry Street runs west of Five Corners, and commercial development extends from Furnace Village toward Mansfield, according to the preservation plan. If you are on that side, see Mansfield. For Easton Center to the northeast, or the whole town, see the service area page.

Permits and verifying a plumber

Repiping or replacing fixtures in an old house is permitted work. The state plumbing code, 248 CMR, has the licensed plumber pull the permit and the local inspector check the job. Faucet or valve leak repairs and clearing a stoppage are exempt under 248 CMR 3.05.

Verify any plumber's license through the Massachusetts Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters before work starts.
