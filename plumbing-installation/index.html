What counts as plumbing installation

Installation covers any work that adds or changes plumbing in the house, as opposed to fixing something that broke. A new vanity, a basement bathroom, a laundry hookup in a mudroom and a kitchen island sink are all installation jobs. So is replacing a tub with a shower, or running a line to a detached garage.

The work falls into three groups. Fixtures are the visible pieces, such as sinks, toilets, tubs, showers and appliances. Supply lines carry water in. Drain, waste and vent lines carry it out and keep the pipes breathing. A job can touch one group or all three, and the vent side is where amateur work usually goes wrong.

Common installation jobs

New fixtures

Sinks, faucets, tubs, showers and toilets set in a space that already has lines. The plumber connects supply and drain, tests for leaks and checks the shutoffs. For toilets and faucets, see toilet repair and faucet repair.

New lines

Running supply and drain to a new location, such as a second bath or a detached garage. This is where venting, pipe slope and fire stopping get serious.

Remodel rough-in

The plumbing that goes into open walls and floors before drywall. It sets where every fixture will land, so it is worth getting right when the framing is exposed.

Remodel rough-in: why timing matters

Rough-in happens after framing and before insulation and drywall. The plumber sets drain lines, vents and supply stub-outs at the heights and spacing the fixtures need, and the work is then inspected while it is still visible. Once the wall is closed, any mistake means opening it again.

Bring the fixture list to the planning call. A wall-hung toilet needs a carrier inside the wall. A freestanding tub needs a floor drain in a specific spot. A double vanity changes the supply layout. Choosing fixtures first lets the plumber rough them in to the exact dimensions on the spec sheets.

Permits and licensing in Massachusetts

Massachusetts requires a permit from the local plumbing inspector before plumbing is installed, altered, removed or replaced. The rules sit in 248 CMR, the Massachusetts Uniform State Plumbing Code, and only a licensed plumber can pull the permit. Some small repairs are exempt, such as fixing a leak in a faucet or valve and clearing a stoppage, but new installation is not one of them.

That is more than paperwork. The inspection is a second set of eyes on venting, trap arms and pipe supports, and it protects the home when you sell. Ask the Easton building department which forms and inspections it uses for your job, since local steps can vary.

How to verify a plumber

Plumbers are licensed by the Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters, part of the state Division of Professional Licensure. You can look up a license on the state's online lookup before you hire. If anyone offers to skip the permit to save money, step away. Unpermitted work can cause trouble at resale and with insurance.

Installation in Easton houses

Easton includes 19th century village houses, postwar capes and ranches, and newer subdivisions, and each asks something different of a new line. In an older house, the existing drain stack may be cast iron or galvanized, and tying a new fixture into it takes care. A plumber will often look at the old pipe first, and if it is tired, repiping can be part of the plan.

Winter is the local design constraint. Putting supply lines in an exterior wall or an unheated crawlspace invites freezing in a cold snap, so lines are usually kept on the warm side of the insulation. Ask your plumber how they plan to protect new runs. And if the house is on septic, new fixtures add load, so the system's size and condition should be part of the conversation.
