Septic or sewer: the first question on a Norton emergency call

Town planning material for Norton describes most residents as running private Title 5 septic systems, with a smaller group tied into municipal sewer. Much of the collected flow goes to the wastewater facility at Crane and Hill Street, and a few properties connect elsewhere. So when a Norton toilet will not flush or a tub gurgles, the first thing a plumber needs to know is whether the house has a town sewer connection or a tank and leach field.

That answer changes the repair. On sewer, a clog in the house line is usually a job for cable or drain cleaning. On septic, the same symptom can mean a full tank, a blocked outlet baffle or a saturated leach field, and running a cable without asking can push the problem further out. Not sure which you have? Look for a yard cleanout cap or ask your septic pumper.

Well-fed water and the plumbing it touches

Norton's public water comes entirely from groundwater. The state water permit for the Norton Water Department lists its production wells along Plain Street, and it notes that one older well has been taken out of service for poor water quality. Local news coverage in past years also reported high iron and manganese in the town supply, which stain fixtures and settle in pipes.

Minerals like that tend to collect in aerators, toilet fill valves and the bottom of a heater tank. A heater that rumbles or runs out of hot water early may be coated in sediment, which is a job for water heater repair before it becomes a leak. Read the Norton Water Department's current annual water quality report for what is in the tap today, and test a private well separately if you have one.

Emergencies we see most often in 02766

Any of these can start at two in the morning, and none of them improves by waiting until the office opens.

Sewage backup in a basement

A floor drain that bubbles or a laundry tub that fills when someone flushes is a house-line or tank problem. Stop using water and call the 24/7 line before the level reaches an outlet.

Sump pump that quits in a storm

Norton ground stays wet in spring and after heavy rain, and a dead pump or a stuck float lets water onto the slab quickly. See sump pump repair for what the visit covers.

Frozen or split supply line

A line in an exterior wall or an unheated crawlspace can freeze during a December to February cold snap. Shut off the main and call, because the thaw is when the water arrives.

No hot water or a leaking tank

Water pooling around a heater is an emergency, and a tank that fails will not repair itself. Our plumbers handle both repair and replacement.

Hidden leak under a slab or wall

A meter that moves with everything off, or a damp patch that keeps returning, calls for leak detection before more flooring is ruined.

Supply pipe failure at the street side

A wet lawn or sudden low pressure can mean the line from the main or well has let go. See water line repair.

Before the plumber reaches your Norton home

A few minutes of preparation keeps damage down and lets the visit start with the repair instead of the search.

Find your shutoffs

Locate the main water valve for a town supply or the pressure tank valve on a private well. Turn it off if water is running where it should not.

Know your waste system

Tell us whether you are on town sewer or septic. If septic, mention the last pump-out if you remember it, and keep the tank lid location in mind.

Clear the work area

Move storage away from the basement drain, the heater or the sump pit so the plumber can reach it.

Protect what matters

Lift boxes, rugs and electronics off a wet floor and stay clear of outlets near standing water.

Photograph the damage

Take photos before cleanup in case you file a homeowners claim. Ask your insurer what it covers.

The dispatcher will also ask about pets, gate codes and parking so the crew can get in. Calls to our line may be recorded.

Low, wet ground and what it does to Norton basements

The regional planning agency counts hundreds of certified vernal pools in Norton, and the town is described as generally low and swampy, with the Canoe River and the Wading River draining it. That is a lot of seasonal water sitting near foundations. A cellar that stays dry in August can take on water in March.

For a homeowner, that means the sump pit, the discharge line and the check valve are worth testing before the wet season, not after the first flood. If water keeps finding its way in even with a working pump, basement waterproofing looks at the grade and the foundation instead of just the pump. Nothing here requires guessing, since a plumber can watch the pit fill and tell you what is happening.

Homes, Wheaton College and the plumbing behind them

Norton is a mostly residential town of about 19,000 people, with older houses around Norton Center and a long run of postwar and later homes built on larger lots. Wheaton College sits in town too, and large buildings of any age carry their own plumbing needs, from boiler rooms to long drain runs. If you manage a small commercial space near Route 140, ask about plumbing installation and repair work as well.

Older homes may still have galvanized steel supply lines that close up with mineral scale, and cast iron drains that crack or belly with age. A newer house on a well and septic system has fewer old pipes but more equipment to maintain. We tell you what we see in either case before any work begins.

Permits and licensing for plumbing work in Norton

Massachusetts requires a plumbing permit from the local plumbing inspector before most plumbing is installed, altered, replaced or repaired, and only a licensed plumber can pull it. Simple jobs such as clearing a stoppage or fixing a leaking faucet are exempt. For anything larger, ask the Norton building department which permit applies.

You can verify any plumber's license on the Massachusetts Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters lookup before they start. We encourage that check with every contractor you hire. Septic repairs, as opposed to plumbing inside the house, are handled separately by the local board of health under Title 5.
