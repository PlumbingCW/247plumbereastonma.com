Failed tank: what to do first and when replacement is the answer

A water heater is a steel tank with a glass lining, and the lining wears out. When the shell leaks or the bottom is thick with hardened scale, there is nothing left to repair. A new heating element in a rotted tank is money spent on the wrong part.

Age matters, but condition matters more. Tanks commonly last about 10 to 15 years, and the best way to know where yours stands is to look at the label for the date, check for rust at the base and listen for rumbling when it heats. If you are unsure, our repair assessment can tell you which way it leans.

Signs it is time to replace the tank

Any one of these is worth a call. Two or three together make the decision for you.

Replacement indicators

  • Water pooling around the base that comes from the tank itself
  • Rust-colored hot water at every tap
  • A tank older than about 10 to 15 years that now fails regularly
  • Loud popping or rumbling that returns right after a flush
  • Repeated repairs within a short time
  • A relief valve that discharges and then the base starts to weep

A single failed element or thermostat usually does not mean the tank is finished. A plumber should show you the evidence before recommending a swap.

Gas or electric: safety before the swap

If you smell gas at any time, leave the house, call your gas utility's emergency number or 911 from outside, and wait for clearance. We will not start work until the gas side is confirmed safe. This applies the same way during an emergency swap at night.

For electric units the first step is the breaker. The plumber confirms the circuit is dead with a tester and does not rely on the label on the panel. Gas units get a pressure and leak test at the new connection, and the flue is checked so exhaust leaves the building and does not stay in the basement.

How a replacement day runs

A like-for-like swap often finishes in a single visit, and a change of fuel or venting can take longer.

1

Shut down and drain

Power or gas and the cold supply are shut off, then the tank is drained to a floor drain or outside.

2

Disconnect and remove

Water lines, flue and gas or electrical connections come off and the old tank is carried out.

3

Set and connect

The new heater is leveled, water connections are made with proper unions or flexible connectors, and a new relief valve and discharge pipe are installed.

4

Fill, fire and test

Air is purged, gas or power is restored, and the plumber checks temperature, combustion and every joint.

5

Permit and paperwork

The licensed plumber takes care of the permit through the local inspector, and you keep the model information for warranty.

Another tank or something different

A replacement is the best moment to rethink the setup, because the plumber already has everything opened up.

Like-for-like tank

  • Fastest swap with the least change to piping and venting
  • Fits the existing space and connections
  • Familiar to maintain
  • Holds a limited supply, so back-to-back showers can run out

Tankless or hybrid upgrade

  • Heats on demand, so there is no standby tank to keep warm
  • Frees floor space on a wall-mounted unit
  • May need new gas line sizing, venting or a dedicated circuit
  • Has a higher install scope, covered on our tankless page

A brand-new install in a home that never had a heater is covered under water heater installation.

Permits and inspection in Massachusetts

Replacing a water heater counts as replacing a plumbing fixture, so under 248 CMR it needs a permit issued by the local plumbing inspector. Only a licensed plumber can pull it, and for gas appliances a licensed gas fitter does the equivalent. In an emergency the regulation allows temporary use once testing is done, provided the inspector is notified and inspects by the next working day.

Ask what Easton's inspectors require in practice, since each town handles scheduling a little differently. Insist that the plumber who does the work is the one who pulls the permit, and verify that license on the state's online lookup first.

What Easton basements ask of a new heater

A lot of Easton homes put the heater in an unfinished basement beside the oil or gas boiler. That is convenient for flue sharing, but it also means a leak can run toward a finished room or a sump pit. A drain pan and a discharge line that goes somewhere safe are cheap protection and worth adding.

If your heater sits in a garage or crawlspace, winter exposure matters. Insulating the first stretch of pipe and keeping cold air off the tank stretches the life of the new unit. The plumber should talk through placement, clearances and venting before they unbox anything.

Permit rules for replacement

Replacement is permitted work. According to 248 CMR 3.05, a licensed plumber pulls the permit and the local plumbing inspector reviews the job, so ask for the permit number when the work is finished.
