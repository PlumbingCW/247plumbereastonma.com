How a tankless heater differs from a tank

A tank keeps forty or fifty gallons of water hot all day. A tankless unit keeps nothing in storage and heats water only when a tap opens, using a gas burner or electric elements. That removes the standby loss and the risk of a large tank failing, and it can supply hot water for as long as the demand stays within the unit's flow rating.

The catch is the flow limit. If two showers and a dishwasher run together, the unit may not keep up, and the water temperature drops. That is a sizing issue, so the plumber starts with your fixtures and incoming water temperature and not just a brand.

Tank or tankless: the real trade-offs

Neither choice wins everywhere. The honest comparison depends on your house.

Tankless strengths

  • Endless hot water within the flow limit
  • No standby heat loss
  • Smaller footprint on a wall
  • Typically a longer service life than a storage tank when maintained

Tankless drawbacks

  • Larger installation scope, with gas line, vent or electrical upgrades
  • Flow limit when many fixtures run at once
  • Needs periodic descaling in mineral-rich water
  • Parts and repairs can be more specialized

Types of tankless systems

Gas tankless

Most whole-house tankless units in New England homes burn natural gas or propane. They need a gas line large enough to feed the burner, sealed venting that exits the wall, and usually a drain for condensate on high-efficiency models. A smaller old gas pipe is a frequent hurdle.

Electric tankless

Electric units avoid venting entirely, which makes them easy to place. Their weakness is electrical demand, since whole-house models draw a large load and may call for a panel upgrade. They suit point-of-use jobs such as a distant bathroom or a small addition.

Hybrid and heat pump systems

A hybrid, or heat pump, water heater is a storage tank with a heat pump on top, so it is not tankless. It pulls heat from room air and falls back on elements when needed. It is worth a look if your basement has air space and a drain.

Signs a tankless heater needs service

Cold water sandwich or temperature swings

A pocket of cold water between hot bursts can be a flow sensor or scale issue. It is a common complaint and usually fixable.

Error codes on the display

Codes point to ignition, venting, flow or sensor problems. Write the code down before you reset it.

Reduced flow or lukewarm output

Scale inside the heat exchanger or a clogged inlet filter reduces performance. A flush often brings it back.

Clicking with no flame

On gas units this can be ignition or gas supply trouble. If you also smell gas, leave the house and call your gas utility or 911.

Water hot at the unit but weak at taps

That can point to partially closed isolation valves or blocked filters, not the burner.

Short cycling

The unit turns on and off rapidly at low flow. It may need a minimum-flow adjustment or fixture changes.

How a descaling flush works

Maintenance is what makes a tankless unit last, and it is the part most owners skip.

1

Shut off power and gas

The unit is turned off, and the water isolation valves are closed.

2

Connect the flush kit

A small pump and hoses connect to the service valves so descaling solution circulates through the heat exchanger.

3

Circulate the solution

Circulation time follows the manufacturer's instructions for your model.

4

Rinse and clean the filter

Fresh water flushes the solution out, and the inlet screen is cleaned.

5

Restore and test

Valves reopen, the unit relights, and flow and temperature are checked.

The right frequency depends on your water. Easton's annual water quality report and a simple tap test help show how mineral-heavy it is. Ask us if service valves are missing, because they should be added during the flush.

Venting, gas supply and permits in Massachusetts

A tankless install often needs a larger gas line than the old tank did, plus sealed venting through an exterior wall. Both are permitted work under 248 CMR, so a licensed plumber or gas fitter pulls the permit and the inspector checks the result. If a contractor says the permit is optional, treat that as a warning and verify their license on the state lookup.

Gas appliance work carries its own risk. Combustion air, clearances to windows, and carbon monoxide protection all matter, and a new CO alarm near sleeping areas is a sensible companion to any fuel-burning appliance.

Is tankless a good fit for an Easton house

Older village homes sometimes have a small gas service and no easy wall for venting, which pushes the cost and complexity up. Newer subdivisions usually have an easier path. The outdoor wall where the vent exits also matters in winter, since drifting snow can cover it if the termination sits too low.

Households with high simultaneous demand do better with a larger unit or two units. Low-use households may save little, because the savings come mostly from standby loss. We will tell you if a good tank or a standard installation is the better call.
