Suspected leak emergency: start with the meter test

Shut off every faucet, the dishwasher, the washing machine and the ice maker. Then look at the water meter, usually in the basement or utility room near where the line enters the house. Many meters have a small triangle or dial that spins when any water at all is moving.

If it turns with the whole house quiet, water is escaping somewhere. EPA's WaterSense program suggests a longer version of the same idea: read the meter, wait two hours with no water use, and read it again. Any change means a probable leak.

Signs of a hidden leak

Most hidden leaks announce themselves long before a ceiling stains. These are the clues we ask about first.

A bill that climbs for no reason

EPA says a family of four using more than 12,000 gallons in a cold winter month may have a serious leak. Compare your bill to the same month last year. Your water bill comes from the Easton Water Division, so they can also tell you how your use compares.

Warm spots, damp odor or mildew

A warm patch on a floor can mean a hot-water line is leaking under a slab. A musty smell in a closet or behind a vanity points to a slow drip inside the wall.

Pressure that drops or hissing you cannot place

A line leaking under pressure often hisses faintly at night when the house is quiet. Lower pressure at fixtures farthest from the meter is another clue.

Is it the toilet? Check before you call

A toilet flapper that does not seat is the most common silent leak in any house. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait about ten minutes without flushing. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper or fill valve is leaking, and that is a simple repair covered under toilet repair.

If the toilet is clean and the meter still moves, the loss is somewhere in the supply piping, the water heater connections or the line outside the foundation. That is when a detection visit earns its keep.

Slab leaks and underground lines

Homes with concrete slabs hide their supply pipes under the floor. When one of those pipes pinholes, water can run for months before it surfaces. Acoustic gear picks up the hiss through the concrete, and a thermal camera shows a warm trail if the leak is on a hot line.

Once the spot is marked, the plumber discusses the options with you: open the slab and patch the pipe, or reroute the line overhead. A line that has failed more than once is worth a conversation about repiping. A leak between the street and the house is a different repair, covered on the water line repair page.

What to do first when you suspect a leak

If water is visible or the meter is racing, move quickly and in this order.

1

Close the main valve

Turn the main shutoff clockwise until it stops. If water stops entering the house, the problem is inside the valve's downstream piping.

2

Open the lowest faucet

Draining the system relieves pressure and limits damage while you wait.

3

Protect what is nearby

Move rugs, boxes and electronics off the floor. Stay away from outlets and panels if water is near them.

4

Photograph the damage

Take dated photos for your insurer before anything is dried or removed.

5

Call the 24/7 line

Tell the dispatcher what the meter did and where you see water or stains.

Why leaks hide in Easton houses

Older homes around North Easton and South Easton often have galvanized steel or early copper that corrodes from the inside, and a pinhole there can spray into a wall cavity. Any house built on a slab may tuck supply lines beneath the floor, where nobody sees them. Newer homes carry PEX or CPVC with fittings that can weep at a crimp or a glue joint.

Winter adds its own risk. Pipes in exterior walls and unheated crawlspaces freeze and crack during cold snaps, and the damage may only show when the thaw comes. If you were away during a freeze, check the meter on your return even if nothing looks wet.
