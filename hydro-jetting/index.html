What a jetter does that a cable cannot

A drain cable is a spinning blade on a coil. It bores through the middle of a blockage and leaves a channel about the width of the blade, while the grease and scale on the pipe wall stay where they are. That is why a line can run fine for a month and then clog again in the same spot.

A hydro jetter pushes a hose with a nozzle down the pipe. Water leaves the nozzle in several angled jets, and the rear-facing ones pull the hose forward while the front ones break up the blockage. The spray scours the full circumference of the pipe and flushes the debris down to the sewer or out to a cleanout.

When jetting beats cabling

Neither tool is better in every case. The right choice depends on what is stuck, what the pipe is made of, and how long the line has been trouble.

Jetting is the better call

  • Grease buildup in a kitchen or restaurant line that keeps returning
  • Heavy scale or sludge coating the walls of cast iron
  • Fine roots hanging in the pipe after a cable cut the thick ones
  • A line that slows down again within weeks of being snaked
  • Sand, silt or packed debris that a cable just pushes around

A cable is enough

  • One clogged sink, tub or toilet with an obvious local cause
  • A hairball or a wad of paper close to the fixture
  • A first-time backup in an otherwise healthy line
  • A pipe the camera shows is cracked, offset or collapsing
  • Small-diameter branch lines where a jet hose will not turn

If you are not sure which side you are on, start with a call and describe the history. A line that has been cabled twice this year is a strong hint that the walls need cleaning, not another hole.

Pipe material matters before any water goes in

High-pressure water is safe in sound modern pipe and risky in tired old pipe. The plumber should look inside before deciding, which usually means running a camera down the cleanout or through a pulled toilet.

The camera tells you more than the age of the house does. A 1950s cape can have a healthy cast iron stack, and a 1990s addition can have a belly where the fill settled. What you want to know is whether the pipe will hold up to the work.

Pipes that handle jetting well

PVC and ABS, which most newer subdivision homes use, cope fine. Cast iron with a rough scaled interior is a good candidate too, as long as the walls are still thick and the joints are tight.

Pipes that call for caution

Old clay tile with cracked or shifted joints can let the jet blast soil into the line. Orangeburg, a tar-and-fiber pipe used in the mid-20th century, can deform or split. Thin, flaking galvanized steel and any pipe with a known collapse should be cabled gently or repaired first.

Signs your line needs more than a snaking

Backups that come back on a schedule

If the same drain stops every few weeks, something is coating the pipe or growing back into it. Grease and roots both behave this way.

Slow drains throughout the house

When the tub, the laundry standpipe and the kitchen sink all drain at the pace of a garden hose, the main line has narrowed.

Odor or gurgle at a floor drain

A sour smell from the basement drain or a gurgle after a flush points to partial blockage and trapped air in the line.

Backup relief: what to do before the jetter arrives

If sewage or gray water is already coming up, stop using every fixture. Do not flush, run the dishwasher, or start a load of laundry, because the main line has nowhere to send the water. Keep children and pets out of the wet area and avoid touching standing water without gloves.

Shut off power to a flooded basement only if you can reach the panel without stepping into water. If you cannot, tell the dispatcher so the plumber knows what to expect. Once the line is open, the first job is to restore flow. The jetting that follows removes the buildup that caused the backup.

How a jetting visit runs

The sequence is the same whether it is a house or a small shop, with details that change by line.

1

Find the access point

A cleanout in the basement or yard is the best way in. Without one, the plumber may pull a toilet or open a stack.

2

Look inside first

A camera pass shows the pipe condition and where the blockage sits, so the pressure setting matches the material.

3

Open the line

If it is fully blocked, a cable or the jet nozzle breaks through to restore flow.

4

Scour the walls

The jetter works back through the line, stripping grease, scale and root hairs as the hose retracts.

5

Prove the result

Water is run from several fixtures, and a second camera pass shows the clean pipe if you want to see it.

Easton lines and what tends to build up in them

Easton mixes housing from several eras, so the pipe under one street can be nothing like the pipe two streets over. Older houses in the village areas often have cast iron or clay drains that have roughened over decades. Newer homes usually have plastic drains, which stay smooth but still collect grease and wipes.

Whether you are on town sewer or a private septic system decides the plan. On sewer, jetting a house line is routine. On septic, a plumber should not push debris toward the tank or leach field without checking the system first. Tell the dispatcher which you have, and mention the last time the tank was pumped.

Tree roots are the other local factor. Easton is wooded, and mature trees reach for the moisture and nutrients in a drain line through any loose joint. Jetting clears the root hairs, but it does not stop roots from returning. If the camera shows roots entering at a joint, repair is the lasting answer, so see sewer line repair and the camera inspection page for what that involves.

Standards behind jetting

Jetting pressure and nozzle choice follow the pipe manufacturer's guidelines for the material, which is why a camera look can come first on old cast iron or clay. Clearing a stoppage is exempt from a permit under 248 CMR 3.05.
