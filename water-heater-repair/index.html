No hot water or a leak: what to do first, gas smell included

If you smell gas near the heater, do not touch a light switch, do not look for the leak, and do not call us first. Leave the house, then call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 from outside. We can help once the gas company or fire department says the home is safe.

A leaking tank is a different problem. Shut the cold water supply valve on the pipe above the heater, and for an electric unit switch off its breaker so the elements are never energized while exposed to water. For a gas unit, turn the gas control to pilot or off. Then call the 24/7 line.

Signs your water heater needs attention

Heaters usually give warnings before they quit. Matching the symptom to the likely cause saves time on the phone.

Lukewarm water or short hot supply

On an electric unit, a failed lower element or thermostat is common. On a gas unit, a dirty burner or weak thermocouple can do the same. Both are repairable.

Rumbling, popping or ticking

Sediment builds on the tank bottom and traps steam under it. A flush can help a young tank, but a tank full of hardened scale is often near the end.

Rusty hot water or a damp base

Rust in hot water only points at the tank. Moisture around the base can be a loose fitting or a failed tank, and only a look tells you which.

Pilot will not stay lit

A worn thermocouple or a draft problem is the usual cause. Repeated outages are a reason to have the venting checked, not just the pilot.

Water dripping from the relief valve pipe

The temperature and pressure relief valve is a safety device. A steady drip can mean excess pressure in the system or a valve that has worn out.

Tripping breaker or burning smell

An electric heater that trips its breaker may have a shorted element. Turn it off and leave it off until it is checked.

Repair or replace: how the decision usually goes

Age is only part of it. A plumber weighs the failed part, the tank condition and what the repair would protect.

Repair makes sense when

  • The tank is sound and not leaking from the shell
  • The failed part is an element, thermostat, thermocouple, igniter or relief valve
  • The heater is mid-life and was running well until now
  • Venting and gas connections look correct

Replace makes sense when

  • Water is coming from the tank body itself
  • The bottom of the tank is heavily scaled or corroded
  • Several parts have failed in a short time
  • The unit is well past the 10 to 15 years a tank commonly lasts

Why gas and electric heaters fail differently

Electric heaters are simple. Two elements heat the water, two thermostats control them, and most no-hot-water calls come down to one of those four parts. Because electricity and water share the tank, a plumber shuts the breaker off first and tests with a meter before touching anything.

Gas heaters add combustion, which adds failure points. The burner, thermocouple and gas valve need to work together, and the exhaust must leave the house through the flue. Any repair that touches the gas connection falls under Massachusetts gas fitting rules, so it is done by a licensed professional and not by guesswork.

Permits and Massachusetts rules for heater work

Under the Massachusetts plumbing and gas fitting regulations (248 CMR), most work on a water heater requires a permit from the local inspector, and only licensed plumbers or gas fitters can pull it. Fixing a leak in a valve or adjusting gas appliance controls without altering the supply connection is exempt, which covers many small repairs.

Replacing the tank, changing piping or altering the gas connection is not exempt. For anything beyond a minor part, ask the Easton building department what they expect. You can also verify any plumber's license through the state's online lookup before you let work begin.

Easton houses and what they do to water heaters

Many Easton basements hold a heater that has been there for a decade or more, often tucked beside an oil boiler or a laundry sink. Homes near North Easton's older streets may have mixed pipe materials around the heater, including galvanized steel nipples that corrode and seize when anyone tries to unscrew them.

Cold snaps raise the load on every heater. A unit in an unheated garage or crawlspace works harder in winter, and a pipe stub that freezes can crack right where it meets the tank. If your heater lives somewhere cold, tell the dispatcher, because it changes what we look for.

Code and safety behind this page

Water heater work follows the state plumbing code, 248 CMR, and the manufacturer's guidelines for your unit. If you smell gas, the guidelines are the same everywhere: leave the building and call 911 or the gas utility before anyone else.
