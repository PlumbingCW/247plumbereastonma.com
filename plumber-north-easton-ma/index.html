Where North Easton sits and what it is known for

North Easton is the northern village of Easton, with its own ZIP code, 02356. The Massachusetts Historical Commission describes it as the town's largest village and industrial center, a place that grew after the Ames Shovel Company began operating in the first decade of the 19th century.

The best known part is the North Easton Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1972. According to Wikipedia, it is bounded by Route 138 on the east, Main and Lincoln Streets on the south, Main Street on the west and Elm Street on the north. Inside it are the Ames Free Library, Oakes Ames Memorial Hall, the old railroad station and The Rockery.

Homeowners use the name North Easton more loosely than the district boundary, so we treat Main Street and the village center as the anchor and do not claim exact edges. Stonehill College has its own ZIP, 02357.

Housing around the village center

A state historic survey describes several kinds of houses here. These are the main ones, with the streets the survey names.

Worker cottages from the shovel era

Federal-period cottages stand on Oliver, Mechanic, Lincoln and Center Streets. Greek Revival and Italianate cottages, including a double-cottage type on Elm Street, were built as the Ames works grew.

Side-hall and L-plan houses

The survey places these along Center and Sheridan Streets and north of Main Street. They come from the late industrial period and are usually larger than the earlier cottages.

Four-squares and bungalows

Early 20th century Colonial Revival four-squares and bungalows fill the neighborhood between Center and Sheridan Streets.

That survey describes architecture, not plumbing. A house on any of these streets may have been updated more than once, so a plumber should look at what is actually in the basement before assuming anything.

What older village houses ask of a plumber

A house that has stood for a century or more can show several generations of work in one basement. Some original pipe may remain in places and some may have been replaced. The only way to know is to look at where the water line enters, what the main drain is made of and whether there is a cleanout.

That is why a slow drain in an older home is worth treating as a question, not a certainty. Drain cleaning clears the blockage, and a camera inspection shows the pipe itself if clogs keep returning. If a damp spot appears with no obvious source, leak detection finds it without opening walls first.

What the history means when you call

Mixed streets mean one block can hold a small cottage with one bathroom and a larger house with several. Say which you have, because the repair, the price and the time on site all depend on it. Do not assume the plumbing matches the architecture, since an old exterior can hide a newer kitchen.

If a pipe is leaking, shut off the water at the main and call. Avoid chemical drain cleaners in an old drain line, and document any damage with photos for your insurer.

Water and sewer: what is documented, and what is not

Easton's water comes from groundwater wells run by the Easton Water Division. Its 2024 water quality report lists six gravel-packed wells, more than 170 miles of main and 7,705 active services. It also describes a new treatment plant on Red Mill Road that removes iron and manganese. The report gives no hardness figure, so ask the Water Division or test your own tap.

North Easton has a long history here. The 2008 preservation plan says dense housing on cesspools made private wells unsafe, so in 1887 the village became a special district that took over a water company.

Sewer is less clear. The same plan says that, apart from Stonehill College and a condominium complex off Lincoln Street, properties relied on private septic systems. A 2013 news report describes a town plan for a treatment plant and collection system serving North Easton Village. We cannot tell you which streets are connected today, so ask the town's public works office or Board of Sewer Commissioners, or check your closing records.

Before you call about an older house

A few details save time on the phone and make the first visit more useful.

Useful to know

  • Find the main water shutoff and note where it is.
  • Know whether the house is on town sewer or septic. If unsure, say so.
  • Note which fixtures are affected and when it started.
  • Photograph any stains, drips or standing water before you clean.
  • Mention any addition, bathroom remodel or past repair you know about.

Permits and checking a plumber

Plumbing work in an older house usually needs a permit. According to the state plumbing code, 248 CMR, a licensed plumber pulls it from the local plumbing inspector before most work is installed, altered, replaced or repaired. Repairing a faucet or valve leak and clearing a stoppage are exempt under 248 CMR 3.05. Ask the Easton building department which work needs one in your case.

You can verify any plumber's license through the Massachusetts Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters. Do that before work begins on any house, old or new.
