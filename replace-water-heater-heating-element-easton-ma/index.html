How to Replace a Water Heater Heating Element in Easton

On an electric tank, you shut off the breaker, prove the power is dead with a tester, close the cold valve, drain below the element, swap in an identical part, then refill the tank fully before power returns. A multimeter tells you which element failed.

The short answer: it is a one-hour job with two big cautions

On an electric tank, a burned-out heating element is one of the most common reasons the water turns lukewarm or runs out fast. Replacing it means shutting off power at the breaker, closing the cold supply, draining the tank below the element, unscrewing the old one and fitting an identical new one. Then you refill the tank completely before the power goes back on.

The two cautions are electricity and dry-firing. Test every wire for voltage before you touch it, and never switch the breaker on while the tank is still empty or only partly full, because an element burns out in seconds without water around it. If any step feels uncertain, the licensed plumbers behind our water heater repair line handle this swap every week.

Is the heating element really the problem?

Elements are a common failure point inside an electric tank, but a few other parts mimic the same symptoms. Check these first.

Lukewarm water that never gets hot

Many tanks have two elements, one near the top and one near the bottom. If the upper one dies, you get little or no hot water. If only the lower one fails, you get hot water for a short while and then it turns cold.

A tripped reset button

Behind the access panel there is a red reset button on the upper thermostat. If it keeps tripping, the cause may be a shorted element or a bad thermostat, so pressing it again and again just hides the fault.

Breaker that trips right away

An element that has failed against its metal sheath can leak current to ground and trip the breaker the moment it heats. That is a good reason to run the ground test described below before you buy parts.

Tools and parts to gather before you start

Having everything on the floor beside the tank saves a trip upstairs while the tank sits drained.

Gather these first

  • A replacement element with the same wattage, voltage and mounting style as the old one (read the label on the tank and the stamp on the element)
  • A new gasket if the element is the screw-in type, or the supplied gasket for the bolt-in type
  • A multimeter that reads ohms and AC volts, plus a non-contact voltage tester if you have one
  • A water heater element wrench or deep socket, usually a 1-1/2 inch socket for screw-in styles
  • A garden hose, a bucket and a flat screwdriver for the access panels
  • Dry rags, a flashlight and rubber gloves
  • The tank's owner's manual or the model label, so the new part is a true match

Step by step: swapping the element

These steps apply to electric tank heaters only. A gas or oil tank has no electric element, so skip this article for those and look at gas burner or thermocouple repair instead.

1

Kill the power and prove it is dead

Switch off the double-pole breaker that feeds the heater. Remove the access panel, fold back the insulation, and touch your tester to the terminals. Do not proceed until the meter reads zero volts, because the wrong breaker is a common mistake in older panels.

2

Shut the cold supply

Close the cold water valve on top of the tank. Open a hot tap somewhere in the house so air can enter as the tank drains.

3

Drain below the element

Attach a hose to the drain valve at the bottom of the tank and run it to a floor drain or outside. Drain until the water level is below the element you are changing. For the upper element that can be a partial drain, for the lower one it means draining the whole tank.

4

Disconnect and remove the old element

Label the wires with tape if there is any doubt, then loosen the terminal screws and pull the wires free. Fit the wrench over the element and turn it counterclockwise. A bolt-in style is held by four small bolts instead.

5

Fit the new element

Wipe the opening clean, seat the new gasket, and thread the element in by hand before snugging it with the wrench. Reconnect the wires, press the reset button once, and close the thermostat cover and insulation.

6

Refill completely, then restore power

Close the drain valve, open the cold supply and leave the hot tap running until water flows steadily with no air spitting. Check for drips at the element, replace the panel, and only then turn the breaker back on.

Give the tank an hour or two to reheat before judging the result. If the water is still lukewarm, the thermostat or the second element may be the real culprit.

Testing the element with a multimeter

A meter turns guessing into a yes or no answer. Remove the wires from the element first so the readings are not skewed by the rest of the circuit.

Multimeter checks for an electric water heater element
TestHow to do itA healthy reading means
Voltage at the terminalsAC volts across the supply wires with the breaker on and the wires still connectedAbout 240 volts at the thermostat. Zero means a breaker, wiring or thermostat fault, not the element
Resistance across the elementOhms setting, one probe on each element terminal, wires removedA steady low reading, commonly between roughly 10 and 20 ohms depending on wattage. No reading at all means the coil is open
Short to groundOhms setting, one probe on a terminal and one on the metal flange or tankNo reading (open). Any steady reading means the element is leaking to ground and must be replaced
Thermostat continuityPower off, test the contacts per the manualClosed when the water is cold. An open thermostat can look just like a dead element

The expected ohms can be worked out from the label. Divide the square of the voltage by the wattage, so a 240 volt, 4500 watt element should read close to 13 ohms.

Easton water and what it does to elements

Mineral buildup on the bottom element is the usual reason it fails years before the upper one. The Easton Water Division draws from town groundwater wells, and a 2023 town newsletter reported an iron and manganese treatment plant under construction on Red Mill Road. We have not verified your street's hardness, so read the Water Division's latest annual water quality report or test your tap before assuming anything.

If you pull the old element out crusted with white or brown scale, a flush of the tank while you have it drained is worthwhile. Sediment sitting on the bottom element makes it overheat, so a swap without a flush can leave the new part facing the same fate.

Do it yourself or call a plumber

Many homeowners are comfortable with this job. These lists show where the line sits.

A reasonable DIY swap

  • You are certain which breaker feeds the heater and you own a voltage tester
  • The tank is not leaking and the shell is dry
  • The element wrench turns freely and the wires are intact
  • You can confirm the wattage and voltage on the label

Call a licensed plumber

  • Water drips from the tank body, not just the element opening
  • Wires are scorched, melted or aluminum with brittle insulation
  • The element is seized and the flange is starting to strip
  • The tank is older and you suspect the shell is failing, which points to replacing the whole heater

Massachusetts rules treat plumbing work seriously. Swapping an electric element is an electrical repair on an appliance, but if your tank has other plumbing problems, the work around it may need a permit from the local plumbing inspector.

How to tell the tank itself is the problem

A new element cannot fix a tank that is leaking from its shell. Rust-colored water at the drain valve, puddles under the base that continue after the fittings are dry, and a rumbling noise from baked-on sediment all point to a tank near the end of its life. Most storage tanks age out somewhere in the range of ten to fifteen years, according to the US Department of Energy.

In that case, spending an afternoon on parts is the wrong move. A plumber can confirm it and size a new unit, and the same advice applies whether you live in Easton or in nearby Mansfield and Stoughton.

Never energize an empty or half-full tank

The most expensive mistake in this repair is restoring power before the tank is full. Run the hot tap until water flows with no air, and keep the breaker off until then.
